Hardware-native

Built to use each machine in full.

Most cross-platform engines flatten every console down to what they all share. Pocket3D is designed the other way round. A game gets a renderer for each machine, so the PSP's fixed pipeline, the 3DS's glasses-free 3D screen and the Vita's GXM driver are all there to be used.

One renderer has to run on all three, so it draws with what all three share. Everything above the line goes unused. A renderer for each machine keeps everything above the line in reach.

Compiler-first

One source of truth, a different program on each machine.

The game is modelled once, as Three.js content that runs in a browser. A compiler lowers that semantic scene to each platform, the way LLVM takes one program to many processors. Hardware-native depends on it: each back end is free to emit what its own GPU wants.

Pocket Tokyo

The real city around Tokyo Tower. Pick a line of it.

CityIR

Shadows turn with the sun

PS Vita · GXM

A texture of heights

16 bits a texel. Each fragment compares its own height with one texel.

Nintendo 3DS · PICA200

A combiner stage

An 8-bit texture of shade over the whole city, multiplied in by one of three combiner stages.

PSP · GE

One bit of a palette index

The upper half of each palette is the lower half in shadow. A thread sets the top bit where the ground is shaded.

The city lights up at dusk

PS Vita · GXM

A glow from the last frame

Each building's rooms come on at their own moment. What is bright on screen is blurred and added to the next frame.

Nintendo 3DS · PICA200

Night as light

The lamps' light is one texture over the city. Facades carry a second picture of what their windows emit.

PSP · GE

Two palettes

Every picture has a day palette and a night palette. The GE reads their mix by the hour.

A wall faces the sun, or not

PS Vita · GXM

A normal in every vertex

A Cg program takes its dot product with the sun, then the shadow lookup cuts it.

Nintendo 3DS · PICA200

A compass sector in the vertex

One byte names the way the wall faces. The vertex program picks one of 17 lights by it.

PSP · GE

One draw per direction

Walls are sorted by the 16 directions they face. Each group is drawn with the GE's ambient colour as its light.

One meaning, three mechanisms. CityIR holds the city as meaning, with no GPU in it. A portable engine would carry one renderer to all three machines and turn its features down, or off, where a machine lacks them. Here each back end emits its own data for its own renderer, so the PSP gets moving shadows without a single shader.

Purpose-built engines

Every game gets an engine of its own.

Pocket3D is not one engine that every game has to squeeze into. Each game builds on it with its own scene DSL, its own IR and its own renderers, and all three are tuned to that one game.

Pocket Atlas

Real places, at the best image a handheld holds at 30 fps.

  1. written in its DSLa place kitsigns, glass, water, light fields
  2. compiled to its IRPlaceIRglTF with a note on what each part is
  3. drawn by its renderersGXM, PICA200reflections, rain, 52,000 point-sprite lights
  4. standing onPocket3D

Pocket Maneuver

Wire traversal through a town of 5,400 houses, at 60 fps.

  1. written in its DSLa town generatorbands, blocks, row houses, one strip atlas
  2. compiled to its IRWorldIRcells, levels of detail, a collision world
  3. drawn by its renderersGXM, PICA200, GEruns of cells in one draw, clip groups on the PSP
  4. standing onPocket3D

Pocket Tokyo

The real city around Tokyo Tower, flown over at 60 fps on a PS Vita.

  1. written in its DSLa city from recordsopen city model, roads, elevation

OpenStrike

A round-based shooter on classic maps, locked at 60 fps on a PSP.

Built for coding agents

Making a game is writing code.

The whole workflow is an agent writing Three.js and a scene DSL. There is no editor to click through and no binary scene to edit by hand, so a game, or any rich interactive scene, can be made entirely by coding. Every step after the writing is a command the agent runs.

youThe LED band over the Radio Kaikan shopfront should chase, like the real one.

agentAdded a 32-frame flipbook sign on the band. On the Vita it costs what a still sign does.

kaikan.ts
// Housing: yellow steel, the face covered by the LED bars.
const housing = lib.plain(0xc9a20e, 0.5, 0.2);
w.mesh(box(bw, bh, zf + 0.3), housing, …);

const sign = w.addSign(new Sign("kaikan-band", tex,
  new Color(1.9, 1.7, 1.25),
  { flipbook: { frames: 32, cols: 1, rows: 32, fps: 6 } }));
w.mesh(frameUV(new PlaneGeometry(bw - 0.06, bh - 0.06), 1, 32),
  sign.material, …);

The same sign on a PS Vita, at 30 fps. An example exchange; the code is abridged from Pocket Atlas.
